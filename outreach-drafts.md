Subject: Guest idea — why entry education doesn't predict pay spread (BLS data)

Hi David,

I caught the episode with Paul Reiman on pay transparency and where comp regulation is headed in 2026, and it touches on something I spend most of my time on from a different angle. I run wagelark.com, a reference site built on BLS occupational wage data (the government's OEWS survey figures, not self-reported salary submissions). The pipeline pulls the numbers, then checks them against hand-transcribed spot values from the live BLS pages, so a typo in the data file gets caught by a test instead of shipping to a wage page.

One pattern came out of building around ten of these pages that I think would make a good HR Data Labs topic: entry education level does not predict how wide the pay spread is inside an occupation. Flight attendants need only a high school diploma, and the gap between the bottom 10% and top 10% of earners is over $100,000 nationally, almost entirely from seniority-based union pay scales. Actuaries have an even wider spread, but for a different reason: a multi-year professional exam ladder. Physical therapists, who need a doctoral degree, have one of the narrower spreads on the site. Three occupations, three very different reasons for how far the pay range stretches.

I'd also bring the "same job title, different employer, very different pay" pattern I keep running into (dental hygienists paid more in private practice than in government settings, radiology techs paid the most by federal employers). I stick to what the published BLS numbers actually show rather than giving personalized pay or career advice, which is the same rule the site runs on.

Let me know if that's useful for the show.

Owen Zhang
contact@wagelark.com

Subject: Guest idea — following up on the WageScape episode (BLS pay-spread pattern)

Hi Wendy,

I listened to the episode with Cary Sparrow on moving past outdated comp surveys toward real-time market data, and it's close to what I spend most of my time on from a different corner of the same problem. I run wagelark.com, a free reference site that publishes BLS occupational wage data (median, 10th and 90th percentile, and pay by industry) straight from the government's own OEWS survey rather than self-reported numbers.

One thing that came out of building the site's wage pages might make a good follow-up conversation for HR Empowerment listeners: how wide the pay spread is inside an occupation has almost nothing to do with how much schooling the job requires. Flight attendants, a high-school-diploma job, have one of the widest top-to-bottom pay gaps on the site, over $100,000, driven purely by seniority-based airline pay scales. Physical therapists, who need a doctoral degree, have a much narrower spread. For anyone setting pay bands, credential level and expected pay variance turn out to be two separate questions.

I'd be glad to come on and walk through that, plus the industry-by-industry swings I keep finding for the same job title (dental hygienists paid more in private practice, radiology techs paid the most by federal employers). I stick strictly to what the published BLS numbers show, no personalized pay or career advice, which is the same discipline the site is built around.

Happy to work around your schedule if it's a fit.

Owen Zhang
